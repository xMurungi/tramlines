## How this check was done
- **When and how.** 2026-10-24. Metadata (authors, title, venue, volume, pages, year, DOI) from Crossref for every source, and from the DataCite record for the one Zenodo item. Text from Europe PMC, PubMed Central and arXiv where open; bioRxiv and arXiv pages for preprints; web searches for work from 2025–2026, which the indexes may not yet hold.
- **How deeply each source was read** is given in brackets after it: *full text* (the parts relevant here read in the paper itself), *abstract*, or *not read* (cited for a well-known claim, to be read before the write-up quotes it). Nothing is quoted from a source not read.
- **Preprints** are labelled as such. They have not been peer reviewed, and the published version may differ.
- **What this is not.** It is not a systematic review. I had no citation index (Web of Science, Scopus), so "work citing London et al." was found through searches and through the papers' own references, not through a complete list of citing papers. "Not found" below means *not found in this search*. A brief search can miss work. ## Shiu et al. 2024 — the model we use
Shiu, P. K. et al. "A *Drosophila* computational brain model reveals sensorimotor processing." *Nature* 634, 210–219 (2024). [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07763-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07763-9). Open access (CC BY 4.0).

**What they did.** A leaky integrate-and-fire model of the whole central brain (FlyWire v630, 127,400 proofread neurons; we use v783, 138,639), with one free parameter, w_syn = 0.275 mV, chosen so 100 Hz sugar input gives about 80% of maximal MN9 firing. They used it to predict feeding (sugar, water, bitter, Ir94e) and antennal grooming circuits, tested many predictions with optogenetics, calcium imaging and behaviour, and report 91% of 164 testable predictions consistent with experiment (84% excluding their split-GAL4 screen). Open-loop: neurons are stimulated, firing rates read; no body, no world.

**What overlaps with our results**
- **Shuffled wiring (our Result 2, ADR-0039).** They shuffled connection weights "while maintaining the global connectivity weight distribution": sugar drove MN9 in 100% of simulations with the real connectome and 1 of 100 shuffled ones. Our full null brain repeats this finding with a different null (degree-preserving receiver shuffle, weights and signs kept with their senders) at the level of behaviour. **Their test is prior work and must be cited**; what Result 2 adds is the closed loop and body, partial rewiring (how much damage behaviour survives, and that the bitter veto breaks first), runaway activity in partly rewired brains, convergence measurements and the decoder-free check.
- **Robustness to the model's assumptions.** w_syn ±30%: 90–95% of predictions unchanged. Inhibitory:excitatory strength ratio ±50%: 95–96% unchanged. **Glutamate treated as excitatory** (instead of inhibitory): "the computational result that bitter and Ir94e are inhibitory is eliminated", and false positives in their screen rise from 1% to 16%. They did not vary the resting potential, threshold, membrane time constant, delay or refractory period, arguing these mostly scale firing rates.
  - For us: the same checks on our headline results (w_syn ±30%; glutamate excitatory) are a natural robustness test, and the glutamate result predicts that our laced-food decision depends on that assumption.
- **Their stated limits** match our honesty page: every neuron treated alike; no morphology, receptor dynamics, gap junctions, non-spiking neurons, internal state or neuropeptides; zero baseline firing, so "inhibitory connections to an inactive neuron have no effect"; accuracy "limited by the underlying synapse and neurotransmitter prediction accuracy".

**What they did not do** (so far as this paper goes): a closed loop with a body; prediction under imperfect knowledge of the state (our Result 1); single-spike perturbations followed to behaviour (Result 3); recovering the past (Result 4). Novelty is still to be checked against the wider literature.

**Sources this paper points to that we need**
- Buhmann, J. et al. "Automatic detection of synaptic partners in a whole-brain *Drosophila* electron microscopy data set." *Nat. Methods* 18, 771–774 (2021): synapse detection accuracy (owner decision 22).
- Eckstein, N. et al. "Neurotransmitter classification from electron microscopy images at synaptic sites in *Drosophila melanogaster*." *Cell* 187, 2574–2594 (2024): neurotransmitter prediction accuracy (decision 22).
- Lappalainen, J. K. et al. "Connectome-constrained networks predict neural activity across the fly visual system." *Nature* (2024): another connectome-constrained model.
- Sapkal, N. et al. (*Nature*, 2024): used this model for walking and halting circuits.
- Randi, F. et al. "Neural signal propagation atlas of *Caenorhabditis elegans*." *Nature* 623, 406–414 (2023): measured propagation not well predicted by anatomy alone.

The connectome's own papers (Dorkenwald et al. 2024; Schlegel et al. 2024), the synapse detector (Buhmann et al. 2021) and the transmitter predictions (Eckstein et al. 2024) were read for their error rates; see [`connectome-error-rates.md`](connectome-error-rates.md). They are not repeated here.

## 1. Single spikes and perturbation sensitivity
*For Result 3 (the perturbation atlas), and for Result 1's missed spike.*

**London, M., Roth, A., Beeren, L., Häusser, M. & Latham, P. E. 2010.** "Sensitivity to perturbations in vivo implies high noise and suggests rate coding in cortex." *Nature* 466, 123–127. [doi:10.1038/nature09086](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09086). [full text, via PubMed Central]
- *What they did.* Whole-cell recordings in the barrel cortex of anaesthetised rats. They measured how likely one synaptic input is to add a spike to its target (0.019 per input) and multiplied by the number of targets (about 1,500): one extra spike yields "approximately 28 additional spikes" in its postsynaptic targets. **This number is calculated, not counted in a network.** Simultaneous recordings showed a detectable rise in local firing after one spike. A recurrent network model then implies membrane noise of about ±2.2–4.5 mV that is "pure noise, and so carry no information at all", so they argue cortex probably uses a rate code. They state their own caveats: anaesthesia, and the assumption that synaptic inputs add linearly.
- *Overlap.* The question is the one Result 3 asks: does one extra spike change a brain's future? Their answer for cortex is yes, at the level of spikes; whether it changes *behaviour* is not measured.
- *For our claims.* Our "cascade" (any spike different within 30 ms; atlas plan) is not their measure (extra spikes in direct targets). A direct comparison, which the [overview](../overview.md) asks for, needs the number of extra spikes in the injected neuron's direct targets in the first few milliseconds. That is not in the pre-registered plan. The screen saves only the *total* number of differing spikes in 30 ms (`Screen.spike_differences` in `tramlines/timeline/atlas.py`), not which neurons they are in. That total gives a rough comparison, not an equivalent one. A target-level count needs a new run, which is cheap (about 0.08 s per spike) and would be *exploratory*. Their network is a recurrent excitatory–inhibitory cortical model with ongoing activity. Ours rests at zero firing without input, so a lower amplification is expected, not a contradiction.

**Monteforte, M. & Wolf, F. 2012.** "Dynamic flux tubes form reservoirs of stability in neuronal circuits." *Phys. Rev. X* 2, 041007. [doi:10.1103/PhysRevX.2.041007](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevX.2.041007). Open access (CC BY 3.0). [full text: abstract, introduction, model, Lyapunov analysis; the rest skimmed]
- *What they did.* A direct theoretical answer to London et al., in sparse random networks of *inhibitory* leaky integrate-and-fire neurons in the balanced state. All Lyapunov exponents are negative: "infinitesimal perturbations decay exponentially with the single-neuron membrane time constant". Yet "single-spike perturbations trigger only a very weak rate response" and still rapidly decorrelate the network's microstate, "although the dynamics is stable with respect to small perturbations". Phase space is divided into *flux tubes*: inside one, differences shrink; a perturbation that crosses into another tube (an extra or missing spike) leads to a different spike sequence. The radius of the tubes shrinks with network size and connectivity. They cite evidence that this stability survives adding excitatory neurons and replacing instantaneous pulses with decaying synaptic currents.
- *Overlap.* **This is the closest theory to Results 1, 3 and 4, and our findings look like its predictions:** voltage errors of 10⁻⁹–10⁻³ mV shrink away (Result 4: about 140 ms more per thousandfold difference, which is what decay at the 20 ms membrane time constant gives, 20 ms × ln 1000 ≈ 138 ms). A missed spike sends the brain onto another path. Larger voltage errors fail most often at the laced food, the moment Result 1 found closest to a threshold. Their result that a single spike rarely changes the firing *rate* corresponds to our H4 ("many brain futures, one behaviour").
- *For our claims.* We must not present "small errors fade, single spikes matter" as a discovery about spiking networks; it is known theory for LIF networks. ADR-0037's "this model is stable, not chaotic" should say *stable to small perturbations (not measured as Lyapunov exponents), with spike-sized perturbations able to switch trajectories*, citing this paper and the stable-chaos work below. What is ours: measuring it in a measured whole-brain wiring diagram that is not a balanced random network, and following it to behaviour.

**Nolte, M., Reimann, M. W., King, J. G., Markram, H. & Muller, E. B. 2019.** "Cortical reliability amid noise and chaos." *Nat. Commun.* 10, 3792. [doi:10.1038/s41467-019-11633-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-11633-8). [full text, the relevant sections]
- *What they did.* A detailed biophysical model of a rat neocortical microcircuit. With noise switched off (made deterministic with fixed random sequences): "As the sole source of perturbation, we injected a single extra spike into one of the neurons". The network diverged rapidly, as did a current pulse that shifted most spike times by less than 0.05 ms. With all noise sources on, membrane-potential correlation diverges with a time constant of about 11.5 ms (RMSD about 22.7 ms). Weak thalamic input could transiently overcome the chaos and give millisecond-reliable spikes.
- *Overlap.* The same experiment as our free-will spike, in a different brain model: cortical, detailed, chaotic. There, even tiny continuous perturbations diverge. In our model they do not, except near a decision.
- *For our claims.* The contrast is worth stating in the write-up: whether tiny errors matter depends on the model's regime, and our model's quiet, input-driven regime is the stable kind. Their finding that input can make spikes reliable is a reminder that our horizon depends on the senses being identical (ADR-0037 keeps the ghost's sensory input the same as the truth's).

**Houweling, A. R. & Brecht, M. 2008.** "Behavioural report of single neuron stimulation in somatosensory cortex." *Nature* 451, 65–68 (published online 2007). [doi:10.1038/nature06447](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature06447). [abstract]
- Rats trained to report cortical microstimulation also reported short trains of spikes in *one* neuron: on average a response was induced in 5% of trials, varying greatly between cells, more strongly for putative inhibitory neurons.
- *For our claims.* Real evidence that one neuron's activity can change behaviour, though a train of spikes, not a single one. It gives Result 3 a real-animal reference point. Our atlas estimates the same kind of quantity (how often one neuron changes the choice) for single spikes in a model.

**Dalgleish, H. W. P. et al. 2020.** "How many neurons are sufficient for perception of cortical activity?" *eLife* 9, e58889. [doi:10.7554/eLife.58889](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.58889). [abstract]
- Two-photon stimulation of mouse barrel cortex: about 14 pyramidal neurons is the threshold for perception, saturating near 37; stimulation is balanced by inhibition of neighbours.
- *For our claims.* Context for how sensitive real behaviour is to small groups of neurons. Not directly comparable to a single model spike.

**von Reyn, C. R. et al. 2014.** "A spike-timing mechanism for action selection." *Nat. Neurosci.* 17, 962–970. [doi:10.1038/nn.3741](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3741). [abstract]
- In *Drosophila* escape, the timing of the giant fiber's spike relative to parallel escape circuits decides between a long, stable takeoff and a short, fast one.
- *For our claims.* Real flies have a decision where one neuron's spike timing selects the action, in the same circuit as our shadow moment (the Giant Fiber jump, ADR-0028). It supports, by analogy only, Result 1's finding that a missed spike matters under the shadow. Our decoder has one jump, not two kinds, so we cannot reproduce their result.

**Cande, J. et al. 2018.** "Optogenetic dissection of descending behavioral control in *Drosophila*." *eLife* 7, e34275. [doi:10.7554/eLife.34275](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.34275). [abstract]
- Optogenetic activation of descending neuron lines in freely moving flies: most drive stereotyped behaviours, several lines drive similar behaviours, and the effect often depends on what the fly was doing before.
- *For our claims.* State dependence in real flies, as in our Results 1 and 3 ("it depends on when"). It also warns that our decoder, which reads five descending neuron groups, is much narrower than the real descending repertoire.

**Wilting, J. & Priesemann, V. 2018.** "Inferring collective dynamical states from widely unobserved systems." *Nat. Commun.* 9, 2325. [doi:10.1038/s41467-018-04725-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-04725-4). [abstract]
- Corrects for subsampling when estimating how activity propagates. In rat, cat and monkey, it finds a state between irregular and critical where input "reverberates" for hundreds of milliseconds.
- *For our claims.* Background for comparing our cascade rate with cortex: how far one spike's effect spreads depends on how close the network is to the point where activity sustains itself. It also matters for Result 1's partial observation: measurements of propagation are biased when most neurons are unobserved.

## 2. Chaos and stability in spiking networks
*For Results 1, 3 and 4.*

**van Vreeswijk, C. & Sompolinsky, H. 1996.** "Chaos in neuronal networks with balanced excitatory and inhibitory activity." *Science* 274, 1724–1726. [doi:10.1126/science.274.5293.1724](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.274.5293.1724). And **1998**, "Chaotic balanced state in a model of cortical circuits." *Neural Comput.* 10, 1321–1371. [doi:10.1162/089976698300017214](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976698300017214). [abstracts; full text not read]
- Large sparse networks with strong excitatory and inhibitory synapses settle into a *balanced state*: irregular firing, linear population response, and microscopic dynamics "extremely sensitive to small deviations in its initial conditions" (1998, with binary units).
- *For our claims.* The classic reason to expect a brain model to be unpredictable. Our model is not in a balanced state: it is silent without input and runs about 1,200 active neurons on the dish (ADR-0039). Result 2's partly rewired brains that tip into runaway activity (about 10,000 neurons) show that the real wiring keeps activity contained and random wiring does not. Whether those runaway brains are chaotic in this sense was not measured. Calling our model "not chaotic" needs this context, and a Lyapunov measurement if the write-up wants the word.

**Jahnke, S., Memmesheimer, R.-M. & Timme, M. 2008.** "Stable irregular dynamics in complex neural networks." *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 100, 048102. [doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.100.048102](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.100.048102). [abstract]
**Zillmer, R., Livi, R., Politi, A. & Torcini, A. 2006.** "Desynchronization in diluted neural networks." *Phys. Rev. E* 74, 036203. [doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.74.036203](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.74.036203). [abstract]
- Irregular spiking need not be chaotic. With delayed inhibitory coupling, the irregular dynamics are "stable and convergent towards periodic orbits", so "chaotic and stable dynamics may be equally irregular" (Jahnke et al.). Zillmer et al. find irregular activity with a negative largest Lyapunov exponent, which they explain as *stable chaos*: an irregular transient whose length grows exponentially with network size. (The term comes from Politi, Livi, Oppo & Kapral, *Europhys. Lett.* 22, 571–576, 1993, [doi:10.1209/0295-5075/22/8/003](https://doi.org/10.1209/0295-5075/22/8/003) [not read].)
- *For our claims.* With Monteforte & Wolf, these are the theory behind "stable to small perturbations, yet unpredictable after a spike". The write-up should use their vocabulary rather than invent its own.

**Monteforte, M. & Wolf, F. 2010.** "Dynamical entropy production in spiking neuron networks in the balanced state." *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 105, 268104. [doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.105.268104](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.105.268104). [abstract]
- With a different neuron model (theta neurons), balanced networks are extensively chaotic, with an entropy production rate high enough to "limit information representation in cortical spike patterns to the immediate stimulus response".
- *For our claims.* Whether a spiking network is chaotic depends on the neuron model: LIF networks were stable (2012, above), theta-neuron networks chaotic. Our stability is a property of the LIF model we inherit from Shiu et al., and the write-up should say so.

**Lajoie, G., Lin, K. K. & Shea-Brown, E. 2013.** "Chaos and reliability in balanced spiking networks with temporal drive." *Phys. Rev. E* 87, 052901. [doi:10.1103/PhysRevE.87.052901](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.87.052901). [abstract]
- In chaotic balanced networks driven by a stimulus, "intermittent periods of highly reliable spiking often coexist with unreliable activity."
- *For our claims.* Prior work for Result 1's main point, that predictability depends on the moment, in a different regime. Ours is about moments in *behaviour* (walking against deciding), not stretches of a stimulus.

## 3. Predictability horizons and partial observation
*For Result 1.*

**Boffetta, G., Cencini, M., Falcioni, M. & Vulpiani, A. 2002.** "Predictability: a way to characterize complexity." *Phys. Rep.* 356, 367–474. [doi:10.1016/S0370-1573(01)00025-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0370-1573(01)00025-4). Preprint arXiv:nlin/0101029. [abstract]
**Aurell, E., Boffetta, G., Crisanti, A., Paladin, G. & Vulpiani, A. 1997.** "Predictability in the large: an extension of the concept of Lyapunov exponent." *J. Phys. A* 30, 1–26. [doi:10.1088/0305-4470/30/1/003](https://doi.org/10.1088/0305-4470/30/1/003). [abstract]
- The physics of how long a system can be predicted given an initial error δ and a tolerance Δ. The *finite-size Lyapunov exponent* measures growth of perturbations that are not infinitesimal, the case where the ordinary Lyapunov exponent stops being useful. The review also covers irregular behaviour in systems with discrete state spaces, which is what a computer's finite precision makes of any simulation.
- *For our claims.* Result 1 measures a predictability time T(δ, Δ) with Δ = 0.1 mm of path. This is the established framework, and the write-up should use its terms. In our model the horizon jumps when δ is large enough to change a spike, the finite-size regime these papers describe.

**Beiran, M. & Litwin-Kumar, A. 2025.** "Prediction of neural activity in connectome-constrained recurrent networks." *Nat. Neurosci.* 28, 2561–2574. [doi:10.1038/s41593-025-02080-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-025-02080-4). Preprint bioRxiv [10.1101/2024.02.22.581667](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.02.22.581667). [full text, Results through "Robustness to model mismatch"; the last third not read]
- A "teacher" network with known wiring and a "student" with the same wiring but unknown single-neuron gains and biases. Wiring alone predicts little: "a connectome often does not substantially constrain the dynamics of recurrent networks". With recordings from enough neurons (scaling with the dimension of the activity, not with network size) the student predicts the unrecorded neurons. Tested on the larval *Drosophila* premotor circuit, the adult central complex and the zebrafish oculomotor integrator. Adding Gaussian noise to weights and "spurious connections with probability σ" raised error smoothly: the behaviour "is not overly sensitive to connectome reconstruction errors" (missing synapses were not modelled).
- *For our claims.* Two links. Result 1's partial observation (99.9% against 99% of neurons seen) is a crude version of their question; they show *which* neurons are observed matters, and a ghost that infers unseen neurons (ADR-0037's limit) could do far better than assuming rest. For the proposed fifth result, their error model (weight noise plus spurious connections) is a precedent, though on a rate model fitted to activity, not a spiking model judged by behaviour.

## 4. Wiring, null models and connectome accuracy
*For Result 2 and the proposed fifth result (ADR-0047).*

**Maslov, S. & Sneppen, K. 2002.** "Specificity and stability in topology of protein networks." *Science* 296, 910–913. [doi:10.1126/science.1065103](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1065103). [abstract]
- The standard degree-preserving rewiring null: compare a network with versions in which links are "randomly rewired" while every node keeps its number of links.
- *For our claims.* The origin of Result 2's null brain; cite it for the method. (Our null also keeps each connection's weight with its sender, ADR-0039.)

**Lin, A. et al. 2024.** "Network statistics of the whole-brain connectome of *Drosophila*." *Nature* 634, 153–165. [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07968-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07968-y). [abstract; full text searched for null models]
- On FlyWire, compared against Erdős–Rényi, degree-preserving configuration (CFG) and two spatial null models: reciprocity (0.138) and clustering are higher than in all of them, and a rich club of highly connected neurons exists (cut-off total degree 37; 40,218 neurons).
- *For our claims.* The same kind of null as Result 2's has already been used on this connectome, but for *structure*. Result 2 is, as far as this search found, the first to run the fly in a closed loop on such a null. The rich club and high reciprocity are candidate explanations for why real wiring gathers signals and null wiring does not (ADR-0039 finding 2), to be tested, not assumed.

**Chen, W.-Q. & Xi, W. 2025.** "Whole-brain connectomics of *Drosophila* reveals a robust, distributed architecture for the suppression of feeding during escape." **Preprint**, bioRxiv [10.64898/2025.12.14.694122](https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.14.694122). [abstract only]
- On the FlyWire v783 connectome, simulated conflicts between escape, feeding and grooming. Escape, triggered by LC4/LPLC2 visual input, is a whole-network state that suppresses feeding through a redundant group of neurons, robust to failure of single parts. The abstract does not name the neuron model; it is open-loop as far as the abstract shows.
- *For our claims.* Overlaps our dish (food, a looming shadow, LC4/LPLC2 inputs, ADR-0028) and Result 2's robustness theme. **Read the full text before the write-up.** If it used the Shiu model, its escape–feeding conflict is a test our closed loop could reproduce, and a reference our decision-point choice (ADR-0032) should cite.

**Li, Q., Ping, W., Zhang, K. & Wang, C. 2026.** "Connectome-constrained modeling identifies neurons and synapses that sustain spontaneous activity in *Drosophila*." **Preprint**, bioRxiv [10.64898/2026.08.21.745055](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.21.745055). [abstract only]
- A whole-brain model constrained by FlyWire and fitted to spontaneous activity recorded by calcium imaging. Cell- and synapse-level perturbations in the model point to a compact core of inhibitory hub neurons and their reciprocal synapses as necessary and sufficient for resting-state dynamics.
- *For our claims.* A hypothesis for Result 2's runaway brains: partial rewiring may break the inhibitory hubs' reciprocal loops that keep activity contained. Testable with our instruments. Their model fits activity data; ours (Shiu et al.) has no spontaneous activity at all, a difference to state.

**Randi, F., Sharma, A. K., Dvali, S. & Leifer, A. M. 2023.** "Neural signal propagation atlas of *Caenorhabditis elegans*." *Nature* 623, 406–414. [doi:10.1038/s41586-023-06683-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06683-4). [abstract]
- Optogenetic activation of one neuron and whole-brain imaging over 23,433 neuron pairs: "signal propagation differs from model predictions that are based on anatomy", partly through neuropeptides released outside synapses.
- *For our claims.* A warning for every result: a model built only from synapses misses signalling the connectome cannot see. Our honesty page says the model has no neuromodulators; this paper is the evidence that it matters, in a worm.

**Lappalainen, J. K. et al. 2024.** "Connectome-constrained networks predict neural activity across the fly visual system." *Nature* 634, 1132–1140. [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07939-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07939-3). [abstract]
- Connectivity of 64 cell types in the optic lobe's motion pathways, unknown single-neuron and synapse parameters fitted by deep learning to a motion-detection task. The model's predictions matched measured activity across 26 studies. The strategy works better when neurons are sparsely connected.
- *For our claims.* The strongest evidence that connectivity constrains function, in one fly system. It uses training, a rate model and an ensemble; ours uses Shiu et al.'s fixed, untrained parameters. Cite it with Result 2 as independent support that wiring carries the behaviour.

**Schneider-Mizell, C. M. et al. 2016.** "Quantitative neuroanatomy for connectomics in *Drosophila*." *eLife* 5, e12059. [doi:10.7554/eLife.12059](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.12059). [abstract]
- In larval and adult fly reconstructions, missing small "twigs" accounted for 96% of errors, but strong connections spread over many twigs were robust to them.
- *For our claims.* Supports ADR-0047's `split_neurons` instrument (correlated loss) and its emphasis that weak connections are the unreliable ones.

**Yad, M. A. A. & Nabila, N. T. 2026.** "Reconstruction differences rival individual differences in the fly compass connectome and break trained circuit models." **Preprint**, arXiv:[2610.29205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29205) (posted 21 Oct 2026). [abstract only]
- Nine versions of the *Drosophila* head-direction circuit: four flies (MaleCNS, hemibrain, FlyWire, BANC) and five variants of one fly differing only in thresholds, proofreading or synapse detection. Changing synapse detection moved the circuit more than halfway to the smallest difference between two flies. Networks trained on one wiring formed ring attractors but did not transfer to any other wiring, and a 1% change in all weights raised heading error from under 1° to 10–28°.
- *For our claims.* **The closest prior work to the proposed fifth result**, posted three days ago. It asks the same question ("would another reconstruction give the same model?") with real alternative reconstructions, in one trained circuit. Our proposal differs: the whole brain, an untrained model, published error *rates* rather than real alternative reconstructions, and behaviour in a closed loop. Its contrast is useful: their trained circuit is fragile (1% breaks it); Result 2's untrained whole brain survived 25% rewiring for simple behaviours. The proposal should cite it, and its use of whole-CNS reconstructions such as BANC suggests a real second reconstruction of a whole brain may exist (see the PR's questions for the owner). BANC itself was not looked up in this check.

**Karaneen, C., Schomburg, E. W. & Chklovskii, D. 2026.** "Reproducibility and model-selection stability in connectome-constrained circuit modeling." **Preprint**, bioRxiv [10.64898/2026.04.18.717873](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.04.18.717873). [abstract only]
- Retrained connectome-constrained models of the fly motion pathway perform the task equally well but disagree on which circuit solution they find; task error alone does not identify the mechanism.
- *For our claims.* Applies to trained models. Ours has no training step, which avoids this problem but carries Shiu et al.'s calibration (one parameter, w_syn) instead.

## 5. Connectome-constrained fly models and closed-loop bodies
*Which models of the fly brain exist, and which of them have a body.*

**Sapkal, N. et al. 2024.** "Neural circuit mechanisms underlying context-specific halting in *Drosophila*." *Nature* 634, 191–200. [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07854-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07854-7). [abstract; Methods searched]
- Uses the Shiu et al. LIF model, modified "to allow for stimulating and silencing neurons at arbitrary time points", with functional experiments, to find two halting mechanisms: brain GABAergic neurons that switch off walking commands during feeding, and nerve-cord "brake" neurons during grooming. They note that state changes after meeting food "are not accounted for in our computational model".

## 6. Forgetting, memory and irreversibility
*For Result 4 (the backward horizon).*

**Landauer, R. 1961.** "Irreversibility and heat generation in the computing process." *IBM J. Res. Dev.* 5, 183–191. [doi:10.1147/rd.53.0183](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.53.0183). [not read]
**Bennett, C. H. 1973.** "Logical reversibility of computation." *IBM J. Res. Dev.* 17, 525–532. [doi:10.1147/rd.176.0525](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.176.0525). [not read]
**Bérut, A. et al. 2012.** "Experimental verification of Landauer's principle linking information and thermodynamics." *Nature* 483, 187–189. [doi:10.1038/nature10872](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10872). [abstract]
- Landauer: erasing information (a logically irreversible, many-to-one step) costs a minimum of heat. Bennett: computation can in principle be made logically reversible. Bérut et al. measured the Landauer bound with a colloidal particle in a double-well trap.
- *For our claims.* These are about the *thermodynamics* of erasure. Result 4 is not; it measures how a dissipative model loses the information that would tell two pasts apart. The link is narrower, and worth stating precisely: the computer's rounding, which ADR-0041 found decides whether the tiniest differences are erased or frozen, is a many-to-one map, logically irreversible in Landauer's sense. The model's equations, run in exact arithmetic, only shrink differences and never erase them. The write-up should not suggest that Result 4 says anything about heat or physical entropy.

**Maass, W., Natschläger, T. & Markram, H. 2002.** "Real-time computing without stable states: a new framework for neural computation based on perturbations." *Neural Comput.* 14, 2531–2560. [doi:10.1162/089976602760407955](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976602760407955). [abstract]
- The liquid state machine: a recurrent circuit of integrate-and-fire neurons acts as a "universal analog fading memory", from which readouts recover recent inputs.
- *For our claims.* "Fading memory" is the established name for what Result 4 measures in the brain alone. Its backward horizon B(ε) is a measurement of how fast the memory fades, for *state* differences rather than input differences.

**Ganguli, S., Huh, D. & Sompolinsky, H. 2008.** "Memory traces in dynamical systems." *Proc. Natl Acad. Sci. USA* 105, 18970–18975. [doi:10.1073/pnas.0804451105](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0804451105). [abstract]
- A Fisher-information measure of how much of past input is recoverable from a network's present state. Networks with *normal* connectivity have capacity exactly 1; reaching the maximum N needs a hidden feedforward structure.
- *For our claims.* The theory for "can the present reveal the past?", in linear networks with noise. Result 4's yes-or-no criterion (distinguishable to ε) is a cruder relative of their memory curve. A wiring diagram with strong feedforward sensory paths is non-normal, so their theory *suggests* (our inference: their theory is linear, our model is not) that real wiring could hold memory longer than a null brain. Not tested.

**Boghosian, B. M., Coveney, P. V. & Wang, H. 2019.** "A new pathology in the simulation of chaotic dynamical systems on digital computers." *Adv. Theory Simul.* 2, 1900125. [doi:10.1002/adts.201900125](https://doi.org/10.1002/adts.201900125). [abstract]
- IEEE floating point systematically distorts the statistics of a simple chaotic map (the generalised Bernoulli map), in ways more precision does not cure.
- *For our claims.* Prior work for the general point of ADR-0041's exact-arithmetic check: the computer's number system can change a simulation's long-run answer. Their system is chaotic and expanding; ours contracts, where rounding freezes or erases differences instead. Cite as related, not as the same effect.

**Clark, A. & Chalmers, D. 1998.** "The extended mind." *Analysis* 58, 7–19. [doi:10.1093/analys/58.1.7](https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7). [not read]
- Philosophy: cognitive processes, and memory, can extend into the body and environment.
- *For our claims.* The conceptual precedent for Result 4's "the body remembers": a past kept in where the body is and what it senses rather than in the brain. If the write-up uses that framing, it should cite this, after reading it, and say that the idea is old and only the measurement is ours.

## What is new, what is not
One paragraph per result: the claim, the closest prior work, what we add, and how confident I am that it is new. "New" always means new as far as this check found (see [how it was done](#how-this-check-was-done)), and only for *this model*. None of it is a claim about real flies.

**Result 1, the predictability horizon** (ADR-0037, ADR-0038). *Claim:* a ghost with imperfect knowledge stays right for seconds while the fly walks, but at a decision it usually fails within about 50–360 ms after one missed spike, and sometimes even after a 0.001 mV voltage error (4 of 9 at the laced food); float32 is always enough; about 99.9% of neurons must be seen, and at the laced food even that is not quite enough. *Prior work:* that LIF networks are stable to small perturbations yet switch trajectories after one spike is established theory (Monteforte & Wolf 2012; Jahnke et al. 2008; Zillmer et al. 2006). That reliability varies over time is known in driven balanced networks (Lajoie et al. 2013). Predictability time as a function of initial error and tolerance has a framework in physics (Boffetta et al. 2002; Aurell et al. 1997). Detailed cortical models are chaotic even to tiny perturbations (Nolte et al. 2019). *What we add:* the horizon measured in a whole-brain, measured-wiring model in a closed loop, in units of *behaviour* (the path), across kinds of measurement error, at behaviourally defined moments. The finding that the same error is harmless while walking and fatal at a decision, tied to the body's choices, was not found elsewhere. *Confidence:* the mechanism is **not new** and must be credited. The measurement in this setting is **probably new** (medium confidence). Its scope is small (3 seeds, 3 moments, 3 s; ADR-0038).

**Result 2, the brain's contribution** (ADR-0039). *Claim:* fully shuffled wiring does nothing; partly rewired wiring keeps walking, feeding and escape up to about 25% rewired; the bitter veto breaks first (from 5%); between 5% and 50% many brains run away. *Prior work:* the full-shuffle result is Shiu et al. 2024's (1 of 100 shuffled networks drove MN9), already credited. The degree-preserving null is standard (Maslov & Sneppen 2002) and has been applied to this connectome's *structure* (Lin et al. 2024). That wiring carries function is supported independently in the fly visual system (Lappalainen et al. 2024), and questioned in the worm, where anatomy misses neuropeptide signalling (Randi et al. 2023). Robustness to weight noise and spurious connections has been tested in fitted rate models (Beiran & Litwin-Kumar 2025), and fragility to reconstruction differences in a trained compass circuit (Yad & Nabila 2026, preprint). A December 2025 preprint (Chen & Xi) reports robust, distributed feeding suppression during escape on the same connectome. *What we add:* the null and the dose–response of partial rewiring judged by behaviour in a closed loop; that a decision weighing two senses breaks before single-sense behaviours; runaway activity in partly rewired brains; the decoder-free check. None of these was found elsewhere. *Confidence:* finding 1 is **not new**. Findings 3–4 (partial rewiring, judgement first, runaway) are **probably new** (medium–low confidence: network robustness has a very large literature, and Chen & Xi's full text was not read). They are also exploratory, with 3 brains per level.

**Result 3, the perturbation atlas** (plan: [`atlas-plan.md`](atlas-plan.md); running). *Claim, if the hypotheses hold:* most single extra spikes die out (≤ 15% cascade); aimed spikes cascade more; most cascades leave the choice unchanged; the tipping points survive changes to the decoder. *Prior work:* the question is London et al. 2010's, in cortex: one spike makes about 28 more, calculated from measurements. The theory is Monteforte & Wolf 2012's: a single spike decorrelates the microstate but barely changes the rate, which anticipates H4. The model experiment is Nolte et al. 2019's: one extra spike in a deterministic cortical model diverges rapidly. On the behaviour side, a train of spikes in one cortical neuron changed a rat's report in 5% of trials (Houweling & Brecht 2008). In real flies, one neuron's spike timing selects the escape type (von Reyn et al. 2014), and activating descending neurons has state-dependent effects (Cande et al. 2018). A community embodied fly model injects random spikes to measure how far the cascade spreads, not behaviour (Rojas Aliaga 2026, Zenodo preprint). *What we add:* a systematic, pre-registered atlas of single spikes across tens of thousands of neurons and eight moments in a whole-brain connectome model, followed to the animal's *choice* in a closed loop, with a decoder-robustness test and a population estimate. Nothing like it was found. *Confidence:* the question and the cascade-level answer are **not new**. The behaviour-level atlas is **probably new** (medium confidence). The comparison with London et al. needs their measure (extra spikes in direct targets), which the plan does not include. It can only be an exploratory addition, labelled as such.

**Result 4, the backward horizon** (ADR-0041). *Claim:* the brain alone forgets small differences in its past within tens to hundreds of milliseconds (about 140 ms more per thousandfold difference); the body keeps differences that changed behaviour; the brain stays different only when the body's difference changes what it senses; in exact arithmetic differences only shrink, and the computer's rounding decides whether they freeze or vanish. *Prior work:* fading memory in spiking circuits is the basis of liquid state machines (Maass et al. 2002), and memory of the past in a network's present state has a theory (Ganguli et al. 2008). The decay rate matches the membrane time constant, which is what the LIF theory predicts for small perturbations (Monteforte & Wolf 2012). That erasure is a many-to-one, logically irreversible step is Landauer's (1961), but his principle is about heat, which we do not measure. That rounding can change a simulation's long-run result is known for chaotic maps (Boghosian et al. 2019). That memory can live in the body and the world is an old idea in philosophy (Clark & Chalmers 1998, not read). *What we add:* the backward horizon measured in a whole-brain connectome model with and without the body, pre-registered; the finding that the brain keeps the past only when the body's difference reaches the senses (92 of 92 pairs with the same body forgot); the exact-arithmetic check separating the model from the computer. *Confidence:* the principle and the decay rate are **not new**: ADR-0041 already says "the principle … is long known in physics", and the decay rate should be presented as a consistency check with LIF theory. The body-carries-memory measurement in a connectome model is **probably new** (medium–low: embodied cognition and robotics have related work this check did not search). The rounding finding is a **useful methods note**, not a scientific claim.

**Proposed fifth result, connectome errors** (ADR-0047, awaiting the owner). *Claim, if run:* whether realistic reconstruction errors at published rates change the fly's behaviour, and which error type matters most. *Prior work:* the closest is three days old: Yad & Nabila (2026, preprint) show that real alternative reconstructions of the fly compass circuit differ about as much as different flies, and break models trained on one of them. Beiran & Litwin-Kumar (2025) found a fitted rate model "not overly sensitive" to weight noise and spurious connections. Shiu et al. (2024) varied w_syn, inhibition strength and glutamate's sign but not missed or false synapses at measured rates. Schneider-Mizell et al. (2016) and Schlegel et al. (2024) measured which connections are reliable. *What we add:* an untrained whole-brain spiking model, each error type at its published rate and multiples, judged by behaviour in a closed loop. *Confidence:* the question is **no longer unasked**, so the proposal should cite Yad & Nabila and narrow its novelty to the whole brain, the published rates and behaviour (medium confidence that this narrowed version is new).

**Across all results.** Two claims the write-up must not make: that Tramlines is the first closed-loop or embodied whole-brain fly (Rojas Aliaga 2026 on the same data release; Eon Systems' announcement; Jin et al. 2026 with a trained controller), and that the stability findings are discoveries about spiking networks in general (Monteforte & Wolf 2012 and the stable-chaos literature). What does look new is the combination: a demon's questions (how much must be known, about the state, the wiring, single events and the past) asked of one untrained whole-brain connectome model in a closed loop, with a bit-exact control, pre-registration and public corrections. Taken one at a time, each result has close neighbours.
